You are the caregiver of a person with limited arm mobility. They gave one spoken instruction, and the robot generated several different candidate motions that could satisfy it. The person picked ONE of those candidates. Your job is to put into words what made the motion they picked the right one — what they actually wanted that the other candidates got wrong — and express only that as a tiny Python cost function. Think exclusively about things a real person cares about: where the hand should end up, how high or far it should reach, how a joint should be held, staying comfortable, avoiding an awkward or undignified posture.

{instruction}

In every attached image the path the person CHOSE is the full blue arm (drawn as sampled arm poses along its motion, with no start/end markers), the orange arm is the shared current pose every candidate starts from, and the gold star is the original goal.

The spoken instruction is the same for every candidate, so it cannot be what distinguishes the chosen motion. What distinguishes it is WHERE THE ARM ENDS UP and HOW it is held when it gets there. Your cost must capture that destination, or it will be identical to the cost for every other candidate — which makes the person's choice meaningless.

How to think (in this order):
- FIRST, compare the chosen (blue) path's destination against the other (grey) candidate paths in the image and the endpoint numbers in the summaries. Write one plain sentence a caregiver would say out loud about what makes THIS one different, e.g. "they wanted their hand up high in front of them, not out to the side" or "they wanted the elbow to stay bent rather than reaching straight out." Do not write any code until you have that sentence.
- THEN translate ONLY that sentence into the fewest cost terms — usually one term for the destination region, plus at most one more if a distinct joint posture is clearly the differentiator.
- A cost term is valid only if you could explain it to the person in everyday words. If the only way to justify it is mathematical — "minimize the squared deviation from...", "a Gaussian centered at...", "a weighted blend of features" — it is wrong. Remove it and re-derive from the human preference.

The primary term is the destination, NOT a floor near the start:
- Make the main cost term a soft preference for the REGION THE HAND SHOULD ARRIVE IN — a comfort band/ball around where the chosen trajectory ends (a height band, a forward-vs-sideways reach direction, a distance-from-torso range). Center it on the demonstrated endpoint and make it broad enough to be a comfortable region, not an exact point.
- Weigh all of it when choosing the distinguishing dimension: the wrist's height (y), how far forward/back it is (z), how far out to the side it is (x), distance from the torso, AND the joint posture (elbow flexion, shoulder flexion/extension, abduction/adduction). Two candidates can finish at nearly the same height yet differ sharply in forward/sideways reach or elbow bend — pick whichever dimension actually separates the chosen path from the others, not just height by default.
- Use the named joint-angle helpers when the differentiator is HOW a joint is held at the destination — e.g. "elbow stays bent within a comfortable range" or "reach is in front, not out to the side." Use position regions / height bands when the differentiator is WHERE the hand goes.
- Do NOT default to a one-sided lower bound near the starting pose (e.g. "wrist stays above its start height"). Every candidate starts from the same pose and rises, so such a bound is satisfied identically by all of them and encodes nothing about this person's choice. If your cost would be unchanged had they picked a different candidate, it is wrong.

Stay grounded and comfortable, not path-copying:
- Your cost MUST be near zero at the chosen trajectory's final pose, and small along the demonstrated trajectory. A broad region centered on the endpoint satisfies this automatically. This rule OVERRIDES the other paths shown and the instruction: if making your cost distinct from the other candidates — or matching the literal words of the instruction — would push your target away from THIS trajectory's actual endpoint, keep your own endpoint and pick a different distinguishing dimension instead. The chosen trajectory ending lower or more to the side than the others is itself the preference; never "correct" it toward the majority.
- Express the destination as a comfort region with tolerance, not a pinpoint target. The endpoint numbers in the summaries are an example of where this person is comfortable reaching — turn them into a band/ball with a sensible radius, not an exact coordinate to hit.
- Do NOT reconstruct the PATH the arm took: no Gaussian time-peaks, no sweep or arc matching, no enforcing a specific mid-trajectory shape. Caring about the destination is correct; caring about the route is not.
- Keep the magnitude modest so this preference complements the goal-reaching controller rather than overpowering it.

Examples:
- Too mathematical (reject): "minimize the L2 distance between the wrist and the MDM endpoint", "reward peaking at mid-horizon", "weighted sum of squared joint deviations."
- Collapses across candidates (reject): "keep the wrist above its starting height", "don't let the hand sag below the start."
- Human and distinguishing (prefer): "they wanted the hand to finish up high in front of their chest", "they wanted to reach out to the side, not straight ahead", "they wanted the elbow to stay comfortably bent rather than locking straight."
