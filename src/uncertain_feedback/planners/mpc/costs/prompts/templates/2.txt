How to think (in this order):
- FIRST, write one plain sentence a caregiver would actually say out loud about what the person wants, e.g. "they want their hand up near their face, but their elbow shouldn't stick out to the side." Do not write any code until you have that sentence.
- THEN translate ONLY that sentence into the fewest cost terms — default to one. If a term does not correspond to a phrase in your sentence, delete it.
- A cost term is valid only if you could explain it to the person in everyday words. If the only way to justify it is mathematical — "it minimizes the squared deviation from...", "a Gaussian centered at...", "a weighted blend of features" — it is wrong. Remove it and re-derive from the human preference.
- Express preferences as comfort ranges and gentle limits, never exact targets: "keep the elbow roughly at or above hip height", "don't let the hand drift far from the body", "let the elbow bend within a comfortable range." The numbers in the summaries are examples of what is comfortable for THIS person — turn them into a band, a one-sided bound, or a tolerance, not a pose to copy.
- Use the named joint-angle helpers when the preference is about how a joint is held (elbow flexion, shoulder flexion/extension, abduction/adduction, internal/external rotation); use position regions or height bands when it is about where the hand or elbow goes.
- Do NOT reconstruct the path the arm took. A caregiver cares that the arm ends up somewhere useful and stays comfortable along the way — not that it traces a particular arc or hits a particular point at a particular time.

Contrast:
- Too mathematical (reject): "minimize the L2 distance between the wrist and the MDM endpoint", "reward peaking at mid-horizon", "weighted sum of squared joint deviations."
- Human and usable (prefer): "keep their hand from dropping below shoulder height while they reach", "let the elbow bend comfortably instead of forcing it straight", "stop the hand from swinging out away from their body."

The cost function you generate should ideally also not restrict the arm from reaching its original goal, unless the demonstrated preference would indicate that they are uncomfortable reaching that goal. Here are some guidelines on how to achieve this:
- Your cost MUST be near zero along the demonstrated MDM trajectory, and especially at its final pose. The demonstrated trajectory is by definition an acceptable way to satisfy the correction, so if your cost penalizes it — or its endpoint — your cost is wrong. Before finalizing, mentally evaluate your cost on context.mdm_traj / context.mdm_positions and confirm it is small there. If it isn't, widen or soften the preference until it is.

How to stay goal-safe:
- Penalize only regions the arm should clearly avoid (too low, too far from the body, an uncomfortable joint extreme). Leave the demonstrated region and the goal completely unpenalized.
- Use soft, one-sided, saturating penalties — e.g. penalize only how far BELOW a minimum height the elbow goes. Never build a hard wall, a narrow target, or a second attractor that competes with the goal-reaching controller and pulls the arm away from where it must end up.
- Keep the magnitude modest. This term shares the objective with a goal-reaching term that must stay dominant; a preference that can overpower the goal is a bug, not a feature.
- Make the preferred set broad enough to contain the goal. If your preference would exclude the demonstrated endpoint, the preference is too tight — widen it.

Then ground it in human terms:
- Read the summaries for what this person finds comfortable (reach height, how far the hand goes, comfortable joint ranges) and express the preference as a band, a lower/upper bound, or a tolerance — not an exact pose. Default to a single cost term and prefer the named joint-angle helpers or position bands.
- State the preference the way a caregiver would, e.g. "keep the elbow from dropping too low on the way up", not as a mathematical formula.
